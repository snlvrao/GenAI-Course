Your model from B3 writes English-shaped nonsense and cannot follow an instruction. This module shows why by contrast: you take a model somebody else pretrained and fine-tune it on instructions in about two and a half minutes on a laptop processor with no graphics card.

Watch this first

The ideas, in plain English

Each idea, then a worked example of it.

1

What pretraining actually buys

Pretraining is the same next-token training you ran in B3, done on so much text that grammar and a rough store of facts fall out of it. SmolLM2-135M-Instruct has 163 times your model's parameters and read something like 1.8 million times more text than your corpus. That gap, not the code, is the whole difference between the two models: your tinygpt.py and its architecture are close cousins.

2 trillion divided by 1,115,394 is about 1.8 million. Halve that figure and the conclusion does not move.
2

Why 0.8M parameters and 1 MB cannot get there

Your model did learn. Validation loss 1.674 against 4.174 for guessing uniformly over 65 characters is real learning, not noise. What it learned is the character statistics of 1.1 MB of one kind of text, which contains no instructions, no questions being answered, and not enough English to spell reliably. There is no prompt that fixes this.

Second right: Are break'd Canunison, and then a poor chard. Myself: Let me and beatured seems rest how stopp'd he ere of From and leathey for might!

Real sampled output after 4 minutes of B3 training. Real words, real line breaks, invented speaker names with colons. Also nonsense. Both halves are the lesson.
3

Scaling, in plain terms

Three dials move together: how much text, how many parameters, how much compute. Loss falls smoothly as you turn them, but it falls like a power law, meaning each equal step down costs a multiple of everything spent before it, not an addition. That is why the jump from your model to a small pretrained one took a data centre rather than a longer lunch break.

Example

4

Fine-tuning adjusts a model that already works

Fine-tuning starts from weights that already write English and nudges them. You are not teaching language, you are picking one shape of response out of the many the model can already produce. That is why 15 examples and 143 seconds are enough here, and why no amount of time is enough from scratch.

5

LoRA is a small trained patch

LoRA (low rank adaptation) freezes every original weight and trains two thin matrices beside the ones you choose. Their product has the same shape as the frozen matrix, so it is added straight on top when the model runs. You train 0.34% of the numbers and ship a 1.8 MB file instead of a 270 MB one.

Example
model = get_peft_model(model, LoraConfig(
    r=8, lora_alpha=16, lora_dropout=0.05,
    task_type="CAUSAL_LM", target_modules=["q_proj", "v_proj"],
))
trainable = sum(p.numel() for p in model.parameters() if p.requires_grad)
total = sum(p.numel() for p in model.parameters())
print(f"\ntraining {trainable:,} of {total:,} parameters ({100 * trainable / total:.2f}%)")

measured output: training 460,800 of 134,975,808 parameters (0.34%)

The adapter is a patch, not a model. Without the 270 MB base sitting underneath it, those 1.8 MB do nothing at all.

7

When from scratch is the right answer

Train from scratch when the domain is narrow, the vocabulary is small, or the data cannot leave your machine. A tiny model with the right alphabet beats a general one carrying 49,152 tokens of English it will never use. Your B3 model is already proof that this is a few minutes of work.

Example
Your situationFrom scratchPretrained plus LoRA
20 distinct symbols, one format (sensor codes, chess moves, DNA)fits, your 65-character model is already this shape49,152 tokens of vocabulary you never use
Data must never leave the machine, including the first download4.4 MB checkpoint, nothing downloaded at any pointone 270 MB download, offline after that
Open-ended English from real usershopeless at 0.8M parametersthe only realistic option
Two of those three rows are real reasons to train from scratch. The third is the reason most projects do not.
Worth knowing

Three numbers in this module are not yours. The 2 trillion token figure, the 270 MB download and the thousands of accelerator hours are what SmolLM2's authors report about their own training run, not anything you measured, and you are taking them on trust. Timings vary by two or three times across laptop processors, so read 143 and 155 seconds as an order of magnitude, not a target. Eighty passes over 15 rows sits close to memorisation: the tuned model learns the shape of an answer, and follows() checks only that shape, so a reply can pass the rule and still be false. Three prompts is an anecdote, not a benchmark, and a 135 million parameter model is still tiny by any current standard, so do not read its wins here as anything more than "much better than 0.8 million parameters trained on 1.1 MB".

def follows(text: str) -> bool:
    """Instruction following, written as a rule a program can check."""
    t = text.strip()
    return (0 < len(t.split()) <= 30      # it answered, and it stopped
            and t.endswith(".")           # a finished sentence, not a cut-off one
            and "?" not in t)             # it answered instead of asking back

    tok.apply_chat_template([{"role": "user", ...}, {"role": "assistant", ...}], tokenize=False)

    "?" not in t

    What that code is doing
    r=8
    the rank. The patch is an 8-wide bottleneck between two thin matrices, so the number of trained values grows with r, not with the size of the frozen matrix.
    target_modules=["q_proj", "v_proj"]
    the query and value projections inside attention, the usual place to attach. You could patch every matrix in the model, which costs more and rarely helps at this size.
    if p.requires_grad
    this is what frozen means in code. The original 134.5 million weights still run in the forward and backward pass, they just never get updated.
    The maths, spelled out
    trainable = layers x [ (r x d_model + d_model x r) + (r x d_model + d_kv x r) ]

SmolLM2-135M: layers = 30, d_model = 576, 9 heads of 64, 3 key/value heads so d_kv = 192, and r = 8.

  q_proj patch:  8 x 576 + 576 x 8  = 4,608 + 4,608 = 9,216
  v_proj patch:  8 x 576 + 192 x 8  = 4,608 + 1,536 = 6,144
  per layer:     9,216 + 6,144      = 15,360
  all layers:    15,360 x 30        = 460,800

460,800 / 134,975,808 = 0.0034, which is the 0.34% the script prints. The hand figure and the printed figure agree exactly.

  5. Train: 80 passes, about two and a half minutes

    This is gradient descent in eight lines, the same loop as B3's training script. The differences are that the batch is your 15 rows all at once rather than random windows of a corpus, and that only the 460,800 adapter values receive an update. Watch the loss fall. Then remember what that loss is measured on.

    python
        opt = torch.optim.AdamW(
        [p for p in model.parameters() if p.requires_grad], lr=LEARNING_RATE)
    model.train()
    t1 = time.time()
    for ep in range(EPOCHS):
        opt.zero_grad()
        out = model(**batch)
        out.loss.backward()          # work out which way each number should move
        opt.step()                   # move them a little
        if ep % 20 == 0 or ep == EPOCHS - 1:
            print(f"  epoch {ep:>2}  loss {out.loss.item():.3f}  ({time.time() - t1:.0f}s)")
    print(f"trained in {time.time() - t1:.0f}s")

# measured on a laptop processor with no graphics card:
# 143 seconds of training, 155 seconds end to end with the model already cached.
    What that code is doing
    [p for p in model.parameters() if p.requires_grad]
    the optimiser is handed only the adapter. This is why 143 seconds is enough: AdamW also keeps two running averages per trained value, so freezing 99.66% of the model cuts memory as well as time.
    batch["labels"][batch["attention_mask"] == 0] = -100
    set earlier in the file. Padding is masked with -100 so the model is not scored on empty space, which would otherwise reward it for predicting nothing.
    Watch out:The printed loss is measured on the 15 rows the model is training on, so it always falls. It tells you the optimiser is working and nothing else. Only the held-out prompts tell you whether anything was learned.

"""Checks the B4 mini-project. Run:  python check_compare.py"""
import json, os, sys

F = "compare.json"
fails = []

def check(ok, msg):
    print(("PASS " if ok else "FAIL ") + msg)
    if not ok:
        fails.append(msg)

def die(msg):                      # missing or broken artefact: stop, no traceback
    print("FAIL " + msg + "\n1 check failed.")
    sys.exit(1)

if not os.path.isfile(F):
    die(F + " not found in " + os.getcwd())
try:
    data = json.load(open(F, encoding="utf-8"))
except json.JSONDecodeError as e:
    die(F + " is not valid JSON: " + str(e))
if not isinstance(data, dict):
    die(F + " must be a JSON object with keys prompts, scratch, tuned, judgement")

prompts = data.get("prompts")
scratch = data.get("scratch")
tuned = data.get("tuned")
judge = data.get("judgement")
if not isinstance(prompts, list) or not prompts:
    die("prompts must be a non-empty list of the instructions you sent")
if not isinstance(scratch, dict) or not isinstance(tuned, dict):
    die("scratch and tuned must each be an object mapping prompt to answer")
if not isinstance(judge, dict):
    die("judgement must be an object with keys better and why")

# --- the same three prompts, answered twice --------------------------------
check(len(prompts) == 3, "exactly 3 prompts (got %d)" % len(prompts))
check(all(isinstance(p, str) and p.strip() for p in prompts), "every prompt is non-empty text")
check(len({str(p).strip() for p in prompts}) == len(prompts), "the 3 prompts differ from each other")
check(set(scratch) == set(prompts), "scratch answers cover exactly those prompts")
check(set(tuned) == set(prompts), "tuned answers cover exactly those prompts")

check(len(both) == len(prompts), "both models answered all 3 (got %d)" % len(both))
check(all(str(scratch[p]).strip() for p in both), "no empty answer from your from-scratch model")
check(all(str(tuned[p]).strip() for p in both), "no empty answer from the tuned model")
differ = sum(1 for p in both if str(scratch[p]).strip() != str(tuned[p]).strip())
check(differ == len(prompts), "all 3 answers differ between the models (%d of 3)" % differ)

# --- the judgement, in your own words ---------------------------------------

The script prints `training 460,800 of 134,975,808 parameters (0.34%)`. What are the other 134.5 million weights doing while you train?

Frozen means unchanged, not absent. Every answer still flows through all 135 million weights, which is why a 1.8 MB adapter does nothing without the 270 MB base sitting underneath it.

Your from-scratch model reached validation loss 1.674 against 4.174 for guessing uniformly, yet it answers "List three colours." with Shakespearean dialogue. Why?

It genuinely learned, and what it learned was one small corpus with no examples of a question being answered. The limit is data and size. It is not a bug, and it is not the character-level design: option 4 is the tempting wrong answer.

You have 40 MB of sensor log lines using 22 distinct symbols, and the data cannot leave your building at all. Which is the better starting point?

Narrow domain, small vocabulary, and data that cannot leave the machine are the three cases where from scratch wins. Option 4 picks the right answer for a wrong reason: an 0.8M parameter model trained on log lines will never handle open English.
