In B2 you built a transformer that produces noise, because all 824,897 of its numbers are random. Here you train those numbers on text you choose until the model writes recognisable English, and you learn to read the one number that tells you whether it is working.

Watch this first

The ideas, in plain English

Each idea, then a worked example of it.

1

Next-token prediction is the whole objective

Training has exactly one task: given the characters so far, name the next one. get_batch cuts a window of 128 characters as x and the same window shifted one place as y. Every position in the window is scored separately, so one window is 128 training examples, not one. There is no other objective anywhere in the file.

Example

train_gpt.py, the batch maker

def get_batch(which: str):
    d = train_data if which == "train" else val_data
    ix = torch.randint(len(d) - cfg.block_size - 1, (BATCH,))
    x = torch.stack([d[i:i + cfg.block_size] for i in ix])
    y = torch.stack([d[i + 1:i + cfg.block_size + 1] for i in ix])
    return x, y

what that shift means, for the first 8 characters of the sample corpus:

  given            'F'  ->  predict 'i'
  given           'Fi'  ->  predict 'r'
  given          'Fir'  ->  predict 's'
  given         'Firs'  ->  predict 't'
  given        'First'  ->  predict ' '
  given       'First '  ->  predict 'C'
  given      'First C'  ->  predict 'i'
  given     'First Ci'  ->  predict 't'
y is x slid one place to the left. Those two slices are the entire training signal.
2

What the loss number means

F.cross_entropy returns the natural log of how surprised the model was by the correct character, averaged over every predicted position. Raise e to that number and you get how many characters it was effectively choosing between. Guessing uniformly over a vocabulary of size V scores log(V), which is 4.174 for the 65 distinct characters in the sample corpus, so any score at or above 4.174 means nothing was learned.

3

Train and validation split

split = int(0.9 * len(data)) holds back the last tenth of your text, and val_loss() averages 20 batches drawn only from that tenth. It is the only number that measures prediction on text the model has never seen. Train loss falling while validation loss rises is memorising, not learning, and the train column alone will never show you that.

4

Batch and context size

Your batch size and your context length are numbers you pick in model_config.json, the small file of choices that the Build track reads. The starter file gives you a batch of 32 windows per step and a block_size of 128 characters per window. One step therefore scores 32 x 128 = 4,096 predictions, and the gradient is their average. The measured 1,223 step run made 1,223 x 4,096 = 5,009,408 predictions in 241 seconds. A bigger batch gives a steadier direction per step but fewer steps per minute, and at this scale steps matter more. 128 is also the hard ceiling on how far back the model can look, fixed by the size of the position embedding table.

Example

train_gpt.py, reading the choices you made in model_config.json

MY = myconfig.load()
BATCH, LR = int(MY["batch_size"]), float(MY["learning_rate"])

_shared/tinygpt.py, class Config, whose defaults your model_config.json replaces

block_size: int = 128     # how many tokens of context it can see at once
n_embd: int = 128         # width of the vector carried per token
n_head: int = 4           # how many attention heads per block
n_layer: int = 4          # how many blocks stacked
The context limit shows up again in generate(): idx[:, -self.block_size:] throws away everything older than the last 128 characters.
5

Learning rate

LR, the learning rate, is how hard each step nudges the numbers, and the starter model_config.json sets it to 3e-3. Too small and the loss crawls, too large and it bounces or turns into NaN and never recovers. 3e-3 is high for a transformer and only works because this model is tiny and AdamW rescales the step for each parameter individually, so 0.003 is not the literal distance any single weight moves.

Example
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=LR)   # LR is yours, from model_config.json
_, loss = model(x, y)
opt.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)
loss.backward()        # which way should every number move
opt.step()             # move them a little
backward() computes a direction for all 824,897 numbers. step() applies it. If your loss prints nan, the learning rate is the first thing to halve.

7

Temperature, and why it is nonsense

generate divides the scores by temperature before softmax, then draws a character at random from the result. Below 1.0 sharpens the distribution towards the safest character, above 1.0 flattens it and lets unlikely characters through. Whatever you choose, a model this small trained for minutes has learned which characters follow which, so you get real words, real line breaks and real-looking speaker names with no meaning behind any of them. Both halves are the lesson.

Example

--- temperature 0.5 --- She that thou come to the man in the body of her. CLARENCE: What is shall the sears of all what the cause put of could And the thrust have to see the son which the bear. --- temperature 1.2 --- That vouchmers all of begell'd when be dry. Greaty's yield th, yet, how! thank contey, stay'd summer'd, anown, not agains thine; yeapulaiest the wall dog pleats heir jectain,

Same checkpoint, two temperatures. Low gives cleaner words and duller repetition, high invents more and breaks more words. Neither one says anything.

Worth knowing

This model has 824,897 parameters and sees 128 characters of context. It learns which characters follow which, and that is genuinely all: no facts, no meaning, and no amount of extra training will make it truthful. A validation loss of 1.55 is a real result for this size and still a long way from anything useful. Two honest limits sit in the code itself. The train/validation split is contiguous rather than random, so your validation set is literally the last tenth of your file and will mislead you if your text changes style near the end. And the seed is fixed at 1337, so you are looking at one run, not an average of several. Timings vary widely between machines, and because the budget is a clock rather than a step count, the same command does different amounts of training on different laptops.

  4. The four lines that do the learning

    Everything else in the file is setup. This is the loop, and it is the same loop that trains models a hundred thousand times bigger. get_batch pulls 32 random windows. Calling the model with targets runs the forward pass and returns the cross-entropy against y in one go. zero_grad clears the previous step's gradients, backward works out which direction every one of the 824,897 numbers should move to make this batch less surprising, and step moves them. Repeat a few thousand times and you have a language model.

    python
    while time.time() - t0 < BUDGET_S:
    x, y = get_batch("train")
    _, loss = model(x, y)
    opt.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)
    loss.backward()        # which way should every number move
    opt.step()             # move them a little
    step += 1

# and inside TinyGPT.forward, the line that defines the objective:
loss = F.cross_entropy(logits.view(B * T, -1), targets.view(B * T))
    What that code is doing
    model(x, y)
    forward pass and loss in one call. The view() calls flatten 32 x 128 positions into 4,096 rows so cross_entropy scores them all together.
    opt.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)
    PyTorch accumulates gradients. Leave this out and step N moves in a direction contaminated by step N-1, and the loss goes sideways.
    loss.backward()
    the backward pass. This is where the cost is: roughly twice the work of the forward pass, and the reason a step takes a fraction of a second on a laptop processor.
    opt.step()
    AdamW applies the move, scaled per parameter using a running estimate of each gradient's size.
    Watch out:val_loss() is decorated with @torch.no_grad() and flips the model to eval() and back. Measuring without no_grad would build a gradient graph you never use and slow the run down for nothing.

  7. Write report.py

    The checkpoint holds everything you need to prove the run happened, so the report script does no training. Save this as my-work/labs/lab21/report.py.     python
    """Turn the checkpoint into train_report.json. Run after train_gpt.py."""
import json, math, pathlib, sys

sys.path.append(str(pathlib.Path(__file__).resolve().parents[1] / "_shared"))
import torch
from tinygpt import TinyGPT

CORPUS = sys.argv[1] if len(sys.argv) > 1 else "mytext.txt"

model = TinyGPT(len(blob["stoi"]), blob["config"])
model.load_state_dict(blob["state"])
model.eval()

itos, samples = blob["itos"], []
for temp in (0.5, 0.8, 1.2):
    ids = model.generate(torch.zeros((1, 1), dtype=torch.long), 300, temperature=temp)
    samples.append("".join(itos[i] for i in ids[0].tolist()))

    "corpus_file": CORPUS,
    "corpus_chars": blob["corpus_chars"],
    "vocab": "".join(sorted(blob["stoi"])),
    "vocab_size": len(blob["stoi"]),
    "baseline_loss": round(math.log(len(blob["stoi"])), 4),
    "final_val_loss": round(blob["val_loss"], 4),
    "steps": blob["steps"],
    "history": blob["history"],
    "samples": samples,
    "checkpoint": "tinygpt.pt",

Your text has 92 distinct characters. After training, your model reports a final validation loss of 4.60. What happened?

The random-guess baseline is log(vocabulary size). ln(92) = 4.522, and 4.60 is above it, so the model scores worse than picking characters out of a hat. Overfitting shows as a low train loss with a rising validation loss, not a validation loss above the baseline. Check the learning rate first, then whether the loss ever printed nan.

Your train loss is still falling nicely but validation loss has risen for three readings in a row. What should you do?

That gap is memorisation. The model is fitting the training portion in ways that do not transfer to the held-back tenth. Measured on a 40,000 character corpus, validation bottomed out at 2.018 around step 400 and reached 2.743 by step 665 while train loss fell to 0.901. More steps make it worse, not better.

What does sampling at temperature 0.2 instead of 1.0 do?

generate divides the logits by the temperature before softmax, so dividing by 0.2 multiplies the gaps between scores by five and the highest-scoring character takes almost all the probability. Nothing about the trained weights changes, and the loss is a property of the checkpoint, not of how you sample from it.
